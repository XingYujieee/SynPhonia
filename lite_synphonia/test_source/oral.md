Good morning, everyone! We’re Group 23. Today, we’re talking about how to get the best of both worlds by combining frequency and spatial domain filtering. But the real question we’re trying to answer is: Is MSE actually a good way to tell if a photo looks ‘good,’ or is there a better way?Before we could fix anything, we had to ‘diagnose’ the image. Looking at the original and its FFT spectrum, it’s clear we’re dealing with a mess of noise. We’ve got periodic stripes—you can see those pulses off-center in the spectrum—plus that annoying salt-and-pepper noise. So, we came up with a four-step game plan: analyze the spectrum, clean up the stripes in the frequency domain, refine the details in the spatial domain, and then double-check everything with different metrics.Step one: getting rid of those stripes. We used a Low-Pass Filter (LPF) and played around with the cutoff frequency, D0. At 70, the stripes were still there. At 50, the whole thing got way too blurry. We found that 60 was the ‘Goldilocks’ zone. It killed the stripes and brought our MSE down to 0.0299, but we still had some graininess and slight blurring to deal with.Next, we wondered: could we just use a standard spatial filter and call it a day? We tested three classics. Mean and Gaussian filters were honestly a bit of a disaster—they blurred everything, and Gaussian actually had the worst MSE at 0.0301. The Median filter was the clear winner here, hitting an MSE of 0.0178. It’s definitely the king of handling that salt-and-pepper look.""Since no single filter was perfect, we decided to chain them together. We built a pipeline starting with the LPF and followed it up with spatial filters. The ‘LPF + Median’ combo gave us the best ‘traditional’ result with an MSE of 0.0294. This proved that a two-stage approach is way more robust when you’re fighting mixed noise.So, looking at the numbers, you’d think the standalone Median filter is the champion because it has the lowest MSE, right? Well, here’s the plot twist: numbers can be misleading. As we move into our exploration phase, we’re going to show you why the 'best' ranking on paper can actually look pretty bad to the human eye.This is where things get interesting. MSE is great at counting pixel errors, but it’s totally blind to the actual *structure* of a photo. Our data showed that while the Median filter has a great score, it ‘over-smooths’ everything. It basically turns textures into plastic. This confirmed that we needed a smarter metric, which is why we brought in SSIM.To make our evaluation feel more ‘human,’ we used SSIM. Unlike MSE, which just looks at pixel brightness, SSIM checks for three things: luminance, contrast, and—most importantly—structure. It cares if the edges and textures actually look like they’re supposed to. And man, did the rankings change! The Median filter, our previous ‘winner,’ crashed down to 5th place. Meanwhile, the ‘LPF + Mean’ combo jumped to the top. It turns out, our eyes care way more about structure than pixel-perfect math.Even with SSIM, we hit a wall. All these classic filters have the same problem: they’re ‘isotropic,’ meaning they blur everything equally. They can’t tell the difference between noise and a sharp edge. The Gaussian filter was the worst offender here, proving that if your smoothing isn't ‘smart,’ you’re just going to ruin the image quality.That’s where NLM, or Non-Local Means, comes in. You can see the workflow here. Instead of just averaging the pixels right next to each other, NLM scans the whole area for patches that look similar. On the right, you can see how it works: the algorithm finds areas that match our target patch (the dark blue box). The better the match—shown in darker green—the more ‘weight’ that area gets. It’s basically using the patterns in the image to fix itself, which is why it’s so much better at keeping edges sharp.To wrap things up, our tests confirmed that the ‘LPF + NLM’ combo is the real MVP. It hit the highest SSIM of 0.607 while keeping a very solid MSE. The big takeaway from our journey? In high-quality restoration, keeping the 'look and feel' of the structure is way more important than just chasing a low error number. Sometimes you have to accept a bit of numerical noise to get a result that actually looks real. That’s it for us! Thanks for listening, and I’d love to hear your questions.
